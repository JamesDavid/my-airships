# "My Airships" (Santos-Dumont, 1904) — Design Reference Catalogue

Source: Project Gutenberg #42344 retail ePub. All quotes are from the book and are public
domain. This catalogue drives two things: **GRAPHICS** (what the world should look and feel
like) and **PHYSICS** (how the ships must handle). Each entry ends with the game-facing rule
it justifies.

---

## PART A — LOCALES: the colorful descriptions

### A1. Paris rooftops (the ever-present hazard)
> "The house-tops looked so dangerous with their chimney-pots for spikes."

> "Landing in Paris, in general, is dangerous for any kind of balloon, amid chimney-pots
> that threaten to pierce its belly, and tiles that are always ready to be knocked down on
> the heads of passers-by."

- GRAPHICS: Haussmann blocks must bristle with chimney-pots — they are the *visual signature*
  of danger. Slate mansard roofs, cream stone walls.
- PHYSICS: buildings are hard collision; scraping one costs gas ("pierced belly").

### A2. Bois de Boulogne (the safe green ocean)
> "As soon as I left Paris and was navigating over the forest of the Bois de Boulogne the
> idea left me entirely. Below there seemed to be an ocean of greenery, soft and safe."

> "I had now reached the Bois, where, by a phenomenon known to all aeronauts, the cool air
> from the trees began making my balloon heavier and heavier — or in true physics, smaller
> by condensation."

> "It is better to catch in the tops of trees, as I used to do in the Bois de Boulogne,
> than to risk the perils of the upper air." / "Such accidents I have always taken
> philosophically, looking on them as a kind of insurance against more terrible ones."

- GRAPHICS: dense soft tree canopy west of the Seine; reads as a rolling green sea.
- PHYSICS: **over-forest cooling** — flying over the Bois slowly cools the gas (lift drops
  ~4–5%), exactly as it nearly cost him the Deutsch Prize. Tree contact is *soft*: drag and
  a shake, not a crash.

### A3. The Eiffel Tower (danger and winning-post)
> "The Eiffel Tower was my one danger, yet it was my winning-post!"

> "I might be dashed against the Tower. The impact would certainly burst my balloon, and I
> should fall to the ground like a stone."

> Deutsch Prize run: "I drove the air-ship onward and upward to a height of about 10 metres
> above its summit... bringing the air-ship round the Tower's lightning conductor at a
> distance of about 50 metres."

- GRAPHICS: the Tower is the one landmark visible from everywhere; lattice silhouette
  against the dawn sun.
- PHYSICS: contact with the Tower = instant envelope burst (the only unsurvivable collision).
  The race checkpoint hugs it — reward a tight, risky turn.

### A4. Cloud country (from the spherical-balloon chapters)
> "The sun cast the shadow of the balloon on this screen of dazzling whiteness, while our
> own profiles, magnified to giant size, appeared in the centre of a triple rainbow!"

> "The sun sets the clouds in ebullition, making them throw up rainbow jets of frozen
> vapour... flakes of snow form, moment by moment, out of nothingness... in our very
> drinking-glasses."

> "A cloud passed before the sun. Its shadow cooled the gas in the balloon, which wrinkled
> and began descending, gently at first, and then with accelerated speed."

> In fog: "the balloon itself had completely disappeared. So we had... the strange and
> delightful sensation of hanging in the void without support."

- GRAPHICS: drifting cumulus with *ground shadows*; envelope visibly wrinkles when cooled.
  (Stretch goal: the "glory" — player shadow ringed by rainbow when above cloud.)
- PHYSICS: **cloud shadow = gas cooling = sink** (~7% lift). This is the core weather
  mechanic; the wrinkle is its telegraph.

### A5. Sounds of the earth from the air
> "The whistling of locomotives throws sharp notes. These faint, piercing sounds, together
> with the yelping and barking of dogs, are the only noises that reach one... Human beings
> look like ants along the white lines that are highways."

> Becalmed over Paris: "In a blue sky without a cloud, bathed in sunlight, and with the
> faint yelps of all the dogs of Paris mounting to our ears, we lay becalmed!"

- GRAPHICS/AUDIO: ambient bed = faint dogs, distant train whistle, church bells ("a joyous
  peal of bells mounted up to us. It was the noonday Angelus").

### A6. The illusion of stillness
> "It seems not to be the balloon that moves but the earth that sinks down and away."

> "The earth, instead of appearing round like a ball, shows concave like a bowl... lifting
> up constantly to the aeronaut's eyes the circle of the horizon."

- GRAPHICS: gentle camera; horizon haze that rises with altitude; no HUD shake.

### A7. Night flight (stretch-goal mode)
> "We see a point of light far on ahead. Slowly it expands. Then where there was one blaze
> there are countless bright spots. They run in lines, with here and there a brighter
> cluster. We know that it is a city."

> "A faint curling line of grey. It is a river, with the moonlight falling on its waters...
> rise through the black solitudes of the clouds into a soul-lifting burst of splendid
> starlight."

### A8. Storm (hazard events)
> Nice: "I was, nevertheless, being lifted by an enormous column of air rushing upward.
> While I fell in it I rose rapidly higher with it." (valve open, still climbing!)

> Péronne: "Up there in the black solitude, amid the lightning flashes and the thunderclaps,
> I was a part of the storm... With it there was a fierce kind of joy."

- PHYSICS: thermal columns that override the valve — you cannot fight them, only ride and
  drift laterally out.

### A9. Monaco — La Condamine and the Mediterranean
> "The little bay of Monaco, sheltered from behind against the wind and cold by mountains...
> would make a well-protected manœuvre ground."

> The sea wall: "From the side walk it was only waist high, but on the other side of it the
> surf rolled over pebbles from four to five metres below." (launch hazard!)

> Maritime guide-roping: "The guide rope held me at a steady altitude of about 50 metres
> above the waves, as if in some mysterious way its lower end were attached to them...
> an incessant little tugging toward and away from the waves... infinitely gentle, an
> automatic ballasting and unballasting of the air-ship without loss of ballast."

> "Here in these azure solitudes there were no chimney-pots of Paris, no cruel, threatening
> roof-corners... I had only to hold my course straight in the teeth of the breeze and watch
> the far-off Mediterranean shore flit past me."

> Return: the ship "was grasped as it came gliding slowly... and 'led' over the sea wall...
> as its trainer or the stable-boys grasp the bridle of their racehorse after the course."

> Hidden danger: towed low over the Prince's steam launch, "that smoke-stack was belching
> red-hot sparks. Any one of those red-hot sparks might have... blown balloon and myself
> to atoms."

- GRAPHICS: second scene — bay ringed by terraced heights, hangar with the giant doors
  ("doors so great as these had never been before"), landing-stage platform on piles.
- PHYSICS: over water the guide rope is a perfect stabilizer (drag through water, no snags).

### A10. Dawn Paris — the No. 9 runabout flights
> "I have guide-roped round the Arc de Triomphe and down the Avenue des Champs Elysées at
> as low an altitude as the house-tops on either side, fearing no ill."

> Wayfinding: "All the avenues meeting at the great 'Star' look alike from the air-ship.
> Also, they look narrow. I was surprised and confused for a moment, and it was only by
> looking back to note the situation of the Arc that I could find my avenue."

> "I might have guide-roped under the Arc de Triomphe had I thought myself worthy. Instead,
> I rounded the national monument to the right, as the law directs."

> "So I reached my corner... Two servants caught, steadied, and held the air-ship, while I
> mounted to my apartment for a cup of coffee."

- GRAPHICS: empty pre-dawn avenues; the Étoile as a radial confusion of identical streets.
- PHYSICS: guide-roping *below rooftop level* down an avenue must be viable and fun — rope
  trailing 20 m on the pavement.

### A11. Aerodromes
- St. Cloud (own expense, 1900): 30 m × 7 m × 11 m, sliding doors "could not be made to
  slide" said the artisans — they slid. Surrounded by hazard trenches that cut up the grounds.
- Neuilly St James (1903, "first of the world's air-ship stations"): "a great square tent,
  **striped red and white**, set in the midst of a vacant lot surrounded by a high stone
  wall." Houses seven inflated ships. Exit route: "Mounting diagonally in the air from my
  own open grounds I pass over my wall, the Boulevard de la Seine, and turn when well above
  the river."
- Monaco: 55 m × 10 m × 15 m, each door 15 m × 5 m, 4 400 kg, rolled open by two small boys.
- GRAPHICS: the red/white striped canvas hangar is the player's home base icon.

### A12. Crowds and rescue (the social texture)
- Kite-flying boys at Bagatelle catch the guide rope and run *against the wind* to break
  the No. 1's fall.
- Workmen fetch a ladder in the Trocadéro gardens mid-race ("Did I need anything? Yes; I
  needed a ladder.")
- Paris firemen haul him off the hotel roof; Princess Isabel sends lunch up into the tree.
- The crowd answers "Have I won?" — "Yes!"
- GRAPHICS: small crowds at Longchamps/Bagatelle/aerodrome that gather beneath the ship.

### A13. Longchamps, 12 July 1901 — the one lap he measured
> "Ten times in succession I made the circuit of Longchamps, stopping each time
> at a point designed beforehand. After these first evolutions, which altogether
> made up a **distance of about 35 kilometres (22 miles)**, I set out for
> Puteaux, and after an excursion of about **3 kilometres (2 miles), done in
> nine minutes**, I steered back again to Longchamps." — Ch. XII

- **3.5 km a lap.** A lap round the racecourse as OpenStreetMap holds it
  measures 3,585 m — his figure and the modern outline agree to 2.4%, which is
  as good a check on the traced course as exists. `tracks.js` trims the last of
  it with `LAP_INSET` so the circuit IS 3.5 km.
- **3 km in nine minutes is 20 km/h**, on the No. 5. That is a rare hard number
  for airspeed and it does not match the game: the ships make about 40 km/h.
  See the note in docs/PARIS_1901.md — it is a real discrepancy, not a rounding.

### A14. The Trocadéro, 8 August 1901 — the ballast he did not throw
> "I saw the propeller cutting and tearing at the wires. **I stopped the motor
> instantly.** Then, as a consequence, the air-ship was at once **driven back
> toward the Tower by the wind, which was strong.**
>
> At the same time I was falling... **I might have thrown out ballast and greatly
> diminished the fall, but then the wind would have time to blow me back on the
> Eiffel Tower.** I, therefore, preferred to let the air-ship go down as it was
> going." — Ch. XIII

> "It had already carried me so far that I was **expecting to land on the Seine
> embankment beyond the Trocadero**. My basket and the whole of the keel had
> already passed the **Trocadero hotels**... the end of the long balloon that was
> still full of gas came slapping down on the roof just before clearing it. It
> exploded with a great noise — exactly like a paper bag struck after being
> blown up... I had **made a mistake in my estimate of the wind's force by a few
> yards.** Instead of being carried on to fall on the Seine embankment I now
> found myself hanging in my wicker basket high up in the **courtyard of the
> Trocadero hotels**, supported by my air-ship's keel." — Ch. XIII

This is the whole of scenario II, and for a long time the game had it backwards:

- **The motor was stopped, by him, on purpose.** That is *why* the wind was
  behind him. The scenario used to give you a running motor and then add a tail
  wind to compensate — inventing the one thing and contradicting the other.
- **The ballast is the decision.** Throwing it slows the fall and buys the wind
  more time to put you on the Tower. It is a real trade and it is the only
  control a pilot with no motor has. `tools/check_scenarios.mjs` flies all seven
  choices: nothing thrown puts you on the roofs at 234 m out, as it did him; one
  or two bags reach the embankment he was aiming for; four or more and the wind
  wins, exactly as he predicted.
- **The hotels, not the palace.** He came down in the courtyard of the *Trocadéro
  hotels* — the apartment blocks on the Chaillot plateau. The survey has 55 of
  them with 23–26 m roofs from 400 m out to 200 m out, and then nothing at all
  between the palace and the river: the Place du Trocadéro, the gardens, the
  cascade, the quay. His account and OpenStreetMap describe the same ground.
- **A headwind and a tail wind are the same wind.** Reports #45 and #46 read as a
  contradiction — the pilot said the book gives a head wind, and the game gave a
  tail wind of 18 km/h. Both are true: it fought him all the way home from the
  Tower, and the moment he shut the motor down it carried him back.

## PART B — HANDLING: how the balloons flew

### B1. Wind is a river, not a sea (Ch. VIII)
> "It is not like sail navigation, and all talk about 'tacking' is meaningless... the
> analogy with a river current is complete. We air-shipmen are steamboat captains."

> "The navigator of the air, however, has the one great advantage — he can leave one
> current for another. The air is full of varying currents."

- PHYSICS: ground velocity = airspeed vector + wind vector, nothing more. Wind varies by
  altitude: **"when the wind is against one, one finds less of it in these low altitudes"**
  (Ch. XXII, reason (c) for guide-roping). → wind scales ~55% at ground to 100% aloft.
  Strategy: run high with the wind, crawl home low against it.

### B2. The central truth (Ch. X)
> "The central truth of dirigible ballooning must be ever: **'To descend without
> sacrificing gas and to mount without sacrificing ballast.'**"

- PHYSICS: gas and ballast are permanent expenditures; *shifting weights + propeller*
  (diagonal flight) is the free way to change altitude. The game must make the free way
  the skilled way.

### B3. Shifting weights / diagonal movement (Figs. 3, 8, 9)
> "Pulling in the fore weight would cause the stem of the balloon to point diagonally
> upward; pulling in the aft weight would have just the opposite effect."

> "The utterly new sensation of movement in an extra dimension!... the delight, the wonder,
> and intoxication of this free diagonal movement onward and upward or onward and downward."

- No. 1: two ballast bags on cords (Fig. 3). No. 5 onward: the **guide rope itself is the
  shifting weight**, hauled fore/aft by a pulley line (Figs. 8–9).
- PHYSICS: pitch input tilts the whole ship; thrust along the tilted axis produces climb or
  dive with zero resource cost.

### B4. The guide rope
> Spherical: "so, unballasting the whole system by so much of its weight, stopped, or at
> least eased, the fall." / Snag: "it kept us shaking like a salad-basket."

> Drag price: "when the guide rope drags it acts exactly like a brake... about 5 kilometres
> (3 miles) per hour." And "the brake-like action of the guide rope... grows... in
> proportion to the square of the speed."

- PHYSICS: grounded rope length converts to lift bonus (auto-stabilized low cruise); rope
  on ground adds drag; rope can snag trees (violent shake) — over water, no snag at all.

### B5. Pitching (tangage) — the speed band
> "Pitching... seems always to commence at 25 to 30 kilometres per hour... when one passes
> this speed considerably — say 50 kilometres per hour — all tangage or pitching will be
> found to cease again." Also: "going thus with the wind... I felt no more motion, indeed
> even less."

- PHYSICS: bobbing oscillation amplitude peaks in the 25–45 km/h *airspeed* band, calm
  below and above; headwind flight pitches more than tailwind flight.

### B6. Envelope pressure, folding, and the propeller-vs-wires disaster chain
> No. 1: "The balloon, a long cylinder, all at once began to fold in the middle like a
> pocket knife, the tension of the cords became unequal, and the balloon envelope was on
> the point of being torn."

> No. 5 (fatal chain): "my balloon was losing hydrogen... it caused the suspension wires to
> sag so much that those nearest to the screw propeller caught in it as it revolved. I saw
> the propeller cutting and tearing at the wires. I stopped the motor instantly."

> The cold explosion (valves, and the Severo Pax): "the ascent of a dirigible should always
> be slow and never great, for gas will expand on the rise of a few yards... Up shot the
> 'Pax' higher and higher, and the expansion, the explosion, and the awful fall came as a
> chain of consequences."

> Speed paradox (Fig. 12): "the danger of the swift dirigible is to blow its **tail out**
> rather than its head in" — atmosphere pushes on the nose (relieving it) but *sucks* on
> the stern.

- PHYSICS: pressure meter. Too LOW (gas lost / cooled) → envelope sags → running the
  propeller hard risks fouling the wires (warning, then damage) — throttle down to survive.
  Too HIGH (fast climb / heat) → valves auto-vent (permanent gas loss); ignore a blocked
  climb and you burst. Longer, slimmer ships fold more easily (No. 1 worst, No. 9 egg immune).

### B7. The capricious motor
> Deutsch run: "the motor was actually on the point of stopping... It was to abandon the
> steering wheel for a moment, at the risk of drifting from my course, in order to devote
> my attention to the carburating lever and the lever controlling the electric spark."

  NOTE: "steering wheel" is the 1904 translator's word (Ch. XV). The French has
  *"j'abandonnai momentanément **le gouvernail**"* — the rudder, no wheel, and *volant*
  never appears in the book in that sense. The helm is drawn as a bar worked by cords to
  each side of the rudder; the evidence is set out in docs/HELM.md.

> "There are sounds in its spitting rumble that are intelligible only to the
> long-experienced ear."

- PHYSICS/AUDIO: random sputter events (guaranteed one on the race return leg); tapping F
  (working the levers) restores it; the engine *sound* degrades a second before the power does.

### B8. Sun, shade, rain, and inflation
- Cloud shadow cools gas → sink (A4). Trees cool gas → sink (A2).
- Rain: No. 2 launched wet — "a strong wind gust of the rainstorm doubled it up."
- Imperfect inflation + hot sun (No. 6's last voyage, Monaco): "the rarefied hydrogen was
  able to rush to the highest possible point — the up-pointing stem... for a time, it
  seemed almost to be pointing perpendicularly" → later ships got **silk partitions** to
  stop gas rushing to one end.
- PHYSICS: when gas is low AND pitch is steep, the nose "rears" — an escalating pitch
  instability, unless the ship spec has partitions (No. 9+).

### B9. Landing discipline
> "He who wishes to navigate an air-ship should first practise a good many landings in a
> spherical balloon."; "Were I to give a single word of caution to all dirigible
> balloonists, it would be: 'Keep close to earth.'"

- PHYSICS: gentle vertical contact = landing; hard = damage; very hard = wreck. Helpers
  (or kite boys) catching the rope arrest a moderate fall.

### Game model mapping (src/ships.js)
Each ship = one data object: envelope geometry (length, diameter, shape: `cylinder | ellipsoid
| stubby | egg | slender`), keel type (`basket-long | pole | truss | saddle | minimal`),
propeller position (`bow | stern | both`), plus physics (thrust, top airspeed, yaw rate,
fold-resistance, pressure limit, gas volume, ballast bags, rope length). Prototype flyable
set: **No. 6** (default, the race), **No. 9** (runabout), **No. 7** (racer). The rest are
encoded for future levels.
